# Hyundai Motor Application Global Assessment

*Field note on a global CRM maturity assessment I ran for Hyundai Motor Group's CRM leadership — and how AI-assisted research turned what is normally a three-month consulting engagement into a fraction of the effort.*

---

## The ask

A senior CRM executive at Hyundai Motor Group asked me to run a global assessment of the group's CRM system maturity across country entities — Korea, North America, India, several European markets, Australia, Brazil, Southeast Asia.

The scope was what a top-tier consulting firm would normally sell as a three-month engagement: a dozen markets, live customer-facing systems, service portals in different languages, dealer channels, per-entity data landscapes. Three deliverables were expected — a per-entity maturity score, the implications drawn from the portfolio view, and a concrete set of next-step recommendations for the group.

The typical answer to a request like this is: hire a firm, wait a quarter, receive a slide deck.

I proposed a faster path.

## How I actually ran it

The work broke into three parts: forensic evidence collection, consistent scoring, and portfolio synthesis.

What made the timeline compressible was that I used AI-assisted tooling — Claude Code and internal Slackbot skills — for the parts of the work that normally consume most of the calendar: parallel data collection, live audits of customer-facing surfaces, cross-referencing multiple internal data sources, and drafting per-entity scorecards from a common template.

That let me spend the human time on what actually matters — judgment, interpretation, and the portfolio-level pattern the executive team needed to see.

## The rubric

Every country entity was scored on the same five dimensions on a 1–5 scale (Minimal → Emerging → Practicing → Optimised → Leading):

- **Channel Strategy** — is the customer met on every relevant channel, and are they routed by intent?
- **Information Architecture** — is self-service organized by product, or by the actual verbs customers use?
- **Search Experience** — does the search bar return a link list, an AI-generated answer, or an actionable widget?
- **Service Console Maturity** — do agents pivot between systems by hand, or does the console surface AI-generated summaries and next-best-actions?
- **Agentic Self-Service** — does the customer get an *answer*, or an *action* that resolves the issue autonomously?

Every score had to be grounded in evidence — a forensic audit of the entity's live customer-facing surfaces (the actual Contact Us page, the actual help center, the actual search bar), cross-checked against internal CRM data on their real platform footprint. Not opinions. Not slide claims. What the customer, on their phone, would encounter today.

## What the portfolio view revealed

Reading a dozen entity scorecards side by side is a fundamentally different exercise from reading any one of them alone.

The maturity range across entities was wider than any single stakeholder had previously assumed — and the pattern was consistent across the trailing entities. The same three dimensions bottlenecked almost everywhere: data integration, agent-autonomous handling, and end-to-end performance measurement.

That's not a software problem. That's an operating-model pattern shared across the group.

Which meant the highest-leverage recommendations weren't "buy more platform." They were: replicate what the leading entities already do, distribute internally the playbooks that already work, and prioritize the specific dimensions where the group's collective return is highest.

That reframed the executive conversation from *"is the global rollout done?"* to *"what is each entity's next move, and where does the group get the biggest return for the least new investment?"* — the shift from Roll-out to ROI.

## What the executive said

The CRM executive who commissioned the work described the final deliverable as reaching the caliber of a three-month consulting-firm engagement, delivered on a dramatically compressed timeline. The feedback centered on two things: the depth of the per-entity evidence, and the clarity of the group-level implications and future-task recommendations.

That matters here for one specific reason: it validated the working thesis that AI-assisted research isn't a shortcut. It's a way to spend a small in-house team's time on the work only humans can do — pattern recognition, judgment, strategic recommendation — while AI-augmented tooling handles the parts that were previously the bottleneck: parallel data collection, live surface audits, formatting, cross-referencing.

The engagement wasn't a demo of what AI can do. It was a working example of what a single AI-augmented practitioner can now deliver at a scope that used to require a consulting bench.

## Why this matters beyond the project

The throughline across all five dimensions of the rubric is one movement: from **Answerability** (the customer has to read and act) to **Actionability** (the system resolves it autonomously). Every serious CRM investment eventually points at that axis. Every serious AI investment does too — which is why "Agentic Self-Service" was scored as a dimension in its own right, not a footnote.

And the way the assessment itself was produced — a consulting-grade deliverable at a fraction of the traditional time — is a preview of what AI enablement should look like inside every large enterprise. Not "AI replaces the consultant." Rather, an experienced practitioner with AI in the loop, doing the work end-to-end.
